# Video Script & Retention

## What this skill is

A complete method for writing video scripts that hold attention: hook in the first seconds, visible proof early, one idea per beat, a stated limitation, and one call to action — plus the review step that catches drop-off points before you record.

It covers YouTube Shorts (up to 3 minutes — see "Longer Shorts" below) and long-form video (8–15 minutes). It does not cover filming, editing software, or thumbnail design, except where the script dictates them. For a finished YouTube thumbnail file, use `youtube-thumbnail-design`; for the thumbnail's concept and cover text, use `carousel-thumbnail-design`.

Who it is for: a creator or small team scripting their own videos who wants a repeatable structure instead of writing from a blank page each time.

## Evidence labels — read before quoting anything

- **FACT** — YouTube's own Help text, with the answer named where possible.
- **REPORTED** — a named study or secondary source, with date, sample size and caveats. Observational, not an experiment.
- **UNVERIFIED** — no source found. Never quote as fact.
- **Recommendation** — a working rule derived from the evidence, not evidence itself.

YouTube publishes no search volumes, no ranking weights, no ideal video length, and no "good" retention thresholds. Treat any exact ranking percentage, search volume, or retention benchmark quoted anywhere as invented. Nothing in this skill invents a number: if a figure would strengthen a line and you do not have one, write the line without it.

## Prerequisites

- A single clear promise for the video: what the viewer will be able to do, know, or decide by the end. If you cannot state it in one sentence, the script is not ready.
- Any factual claim in the script must have a source you have opened. No fabricated statistics, results, or testimonials — ever.
- A way to count words (any word processor) and a timer for the read-aloud test.

## When to use

- Writing any Shorts script or long-form outline.
- Reviewing an existing script for retention before recording.
- Checking disclosure and discovery rules before publishing an AI-assisted video.

## Hard rules

- **Hook in the first moments:** an immediate problem or benefit, spoken and on screen. Visible proof in the first seconds — never a talking-head-only open with no demonstration.
- **Never promise automatic monetisation, guaranteed views, viral outcomes, or income.** Promise only what the video actually delivers.
- **AI disclosure:** realistic AI-generated or significantly altered content needs the platform's disclosure — see "Discovery and disclosure rules" below, and check YouTube's current help page before each upload, because the rule changes.
- **Music rights:** voiceover only, or voiceover plus royalty-free music. Never use a commercial song or original clip audio at any volume — low volume does not avoid a Content ID claim. (FACT: a Short longer than 1 minute with an active Content ID claim of any kind is blocked worldwide.)
- **One call to action per video.** One thing the viewer should do next.
- **The on-screen headline and the opening spoken phrase carry the primary search phrase** — the words a viewer would type to find this video.

## Three definitions used throughout

**Read-aloud test.** Read the finished script out loud, at natural speaking pace, with a timer running. The script passes if (a) the read time lands inside the target duration, (b) no sentence makes you stumble or run out of breath, and (c) it sounds like something a person would say, not something written to be read. Any sentence that fails gets rewritten, not performed harder. Run this test before recording — never after.

**Save-worthy asset + future-moment anchor.** Every script must contain one thing worth saving (a prompt, a checklist, a template, a formula — something the viewer can use later) and one line that names the future moment they will use it ("the next time a leaf turns yellow, open this"). The asset gives the viewer a reason to save; the anchor gives them a reason to remember when. If a script has neither, add one before recording.

**Caption adds to the visual, never repeats it.** On-screen captions highlight the key word or phrase the viewer should remember; they do not transcribe everything being said. If the caption and the voiceover say the same words at the same time, delete the caption or shorten it to the keyword. See the caption spec below.

## Hook options — write three, pick one

Before writing the body, write three different hook lines for the same payoff (a
result-first line, a mistake-and-fix line, and a question line — or any three
distinct angles). Read each aloud with a timer: the promise must land by the end
of the second second. Pick the strongest; keep the other two as spares for the
next video. Never open with a greeting, an introduction, or "welcome back".

## Duration math (a rough estimate, not a measurement)

Estimate duration as **word count ÷ 150 words per minute**. This is a rough estimate only: natural delivery varies roughly 130–170 words per minute depending on speaker, pauses, and emphasis, so a 150-word script reads in about 52–69 seconds. Always confirm with the read-aloud test — the estimate plans the script, the timer decides it.

| Target | Word budget at 150 wpm |
|---|---|
| 30-second Short | 60–80 words |
| 45-second Short | 100–115 words |
| 60-second Short | 135–155 words |
| 10-minute long-form | 1,300–1,500 words |

Count **all spoken words** against the budget — including the hook headline if
you speak it. A script that counts only the body and then speaks the headline
runs long. For a Short, hand-count the words. Do not trust any tool's word count
without checking it.

## Shorts structure (up to 60 seconds)

1. **0–3s hook:** problem or benefit, spoken and on screen (3–8 words on screen).
2. **3–15s proof:** show the result or the tool working — no talking-head-only open.
3. **15–45s steps:** visible steps, one per beat, numbered on screen.
4. **45–55s caveat:** one limitation, honestly stated.
5. **55–60s call to action:** one action (save, try the prompt, comment the tool).

## Longer Shorts (60–180 seconds)

(FACT: square or vertical videos up to 3 minutes count as Shorts.) The 60-second
structure above is the core. For a 60–180 second Short, keep the hook (0–3s), the
proof (3–15s), the caveat, and the single call to action in the same positions,
and extend only the middle: add one or two more steps, or give one step a worked
example. Put a re-hook or pattern interrupt every 15–20 seconds and a stronger
re-hook at the two-thirds mark. Never extend by dropping the caveat, and never
let a Short cross 60 seconds while it carries uncleared music — see the music
rule above.

## Shot-by-shot timing table (60-second Short)

One shot per information beat; a shot holds as long as the information needs, roughly 3 seconds or more per shot. Cut on every information change — a new step, a new number, a new visual proof — never on a timer alone. Background movement stays slow; rapid cuts and fast pans strain the eye and break the connection with the viewer. (Competing practitioner cadences cut every 1.5–2 seconds; this skill keeps the ≥3-second floor because a shot that ends before its information lands reads as noise, not energy. If your audience and format need faster cuts, change the floor deliberately and note it — do not drift into it.)

| Time | Beat | What the viewer sees | Spoken / on-screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:03 | Hook | The problem, visual first (e.g. an empty inbox, a stalled screen) | Hook line + 3–8 word headline |
| 0:03–0:08 | Proof, part 1 | The result working on screen | "Here is what changed" |
| 0:08–0:15 | Proof, part 2 | The result, second angle or closer look | One-sentence payoff |
| 0:15–0:25 | Step 1 | Step 1 demonstrated, numbered on screen | Step 1, one sentence |
| 0:25–0:35 | Step 2 | Step 2 demonstrated, numbered on screen | Step 2, one sentence |
| 0:35–0:45 | Step 3 | Step 3 demonstrated, numbered on screen | Step 3, one sentence |
| 0:45–0:55 | Caveat | The limitation, stated plainly on screen | "It won't work if…" |
| 0:55–1:00 | Call to action | The save-worthy asset on screen | One action, one sentence |

For a 30-second Short, compress to hook (0–3s), proof (3–10s), one or two steps (10–22s), caveat (22–27s), call to action (27–30s). Never compress by dropping the caveat.

## Worked example: complete 60-second Shorts script

Topic: three mistakes that kill houseplants. Voiceover: 147 words. Estimated duration at 150 wpm: ~59 seconds. The 7-word on-screen headline is shown in the first 3 seconds; if you also speak it, the spoken total is 154 words (about 62 seconds at 150 wpm) — trim 5 or more words from the steps first. Verify with the read-aloud test.

> **On-screen headline (0–3s):** "Your plant is dying. You did this."
>
> **[On screen 0:03–0:08: the same plant, two weeks later — new green growth. This is the proof visual.]**
>
> **VO:** "Your plant was fine when you brought it home. Now the leaves are yellow, and you have no idea why. Step one: stop watering on a schedule — water only when the top two centimetres of soil are dry. A calendar does not know your plant.
>
> Step two: move it out of the dark corner — most houseplants need bright light that never touches the leaves directly. If you cannot read a book there at noon, it is too dark.
>
> Step three: stop repotting a struggling plant — new soil does not fix overwatering. Fix the water first, then wait two weeks.
>
> One honest limit: a plant with rotted roots cannot be saved by any trick. If the roots are black and soft, start again with a cutting.
>
> Save this for the next time a leaf turns yellow — that is the moment you will need it."

**Check against the structure:** hook 0–3s ("Your plant is dying. You did this."); proof 3–15s (the recovered plant on screen by ~8s; the first fix named by ~12s); steps 15–45s (three numbered steps); caveat 45–55s (rotted roots cannot be saved); call to action 55–60s (save it). Save-worthy asset: the three fixes. Future-moment anchor: "the next time a leaf turns yellow".

## Retention-review checklist

Run this on every script before recording. Any "no" is a rewrite, not a note.

- [ ] **Hook names a problem or benefit in the first 3 seconds.** Not a greeting, not an introduction, not "welcome back".
- [ ] **Proof appears by 15 seconds.** The viewer has seen the result or the tool working, not just heard about it.
- [ ] **One idea per beat.** Each section does one thing. If a section needs the word "and also" twice, split it.
- [ ] **A re-hook or pattern interrupt every 15–20 seconds.** A new visual, a number on screen, a question, a change of shot — something that resets attention.
- [ ] **No dead stretch longer than 5 seconds** without a new visual, a number, or a spoken turn.
- [ ] **Caveat present and honest.** One real limitation, stated plainly.
- [ ] **One call to action only.** Not two, not "like and subscribe and comment and save".
- [ ] **Read-aloud test passed** (time in range, no stumbles, sounds spoken).
- [ ] **Save-worthy asset named on screen** and a future-moment anchor spoken.
- [ ] **Every factual claim has a source.** No claim without one.
- [ ] **Captions add, never repeat** (see the caption spec).

### Worked retention review (applied to the example script above)

| Check | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Hook in first 3s | Pass | "Your plant is dying. You did this." — problem, 7 words |
| Proof by 15s | Pass | Recovered plant on screen by ~8s; first fix named by ~12s |
| One idea per beat | Pass | Three steps, one action each |
| Re-hook every 15–20s | Pass | Numbered steps at ~15s, ~25s, ~35s; the light test at ~30s is a pattern interrupt |
| No dead stretch over 5s | Pass | Every beat changes the on-screen text or visual |
| Caveat present | Pass | "A plant with rotted roots cannot be saved by any trick" |
| One call to action | Pass | Save only |
| Read-aloud test | Must run | 147 words VO ≈ 59s at 150 wpm (154 spoken with the headline — trim first if speaking it); confirm by timing |
| Save-worthy asset + anchor | Pass | The three fixes; "the next time a leaf turns yellow" |
| Claims sourced | Pass | No statistics or factual claims made — advice only |
| Captions add, never repeat | Pass | On-screen text is the headline and the step numbers, not a transcript |

Drop-off points this review would have caught if the script had them: a greeting before the hook, a step that runs past 12 seconds without a visual change, or a second call to action ("and subscribe") after the save ask.

## Caption / subtitle spec (1080×1920)

This is a recommended spec for vertical video, not a platform rule. YouTube does not mandate caption styling; these values keep captions readable on a phone.

- **Canvas:** 1080×1920 (9:16).
- **Safe zones:** keep all caption text inside the central safe area — at least 120 px from the left and right edges, at least 250 px from the bottom (the bottom is covered by the video title, buttons, and progress bar in the Shorts player), and at least 150 px from the top. Never place captions under the right-hand action buttons (like, comment, share).
- **Font:** a bold sans-serif (for example Inter Bold, Arial Bold, or Helvetica Bold). One font for the whole video.
- **Size:** 72–96 px cap height at 1080 px width for the main caption line; the hook headline may go larger (110–140 px) if it stays inside the safe zone.
- **Words per caption:** 2–4 words per caption event; one line where possible, two lines maximum. Never one word at a time through function words ("the", "a", "and" alone on screen).
- **Style:** high-contrast fill (white or near-white) with a thick dark outline or a semi-opaque dark box behind the text. Keyword colour only on the one key word per caption.
- **Timing:** captions appear on the spoken word, not before it; each caption lasts as long as the phrase it carries. No caption event overlaps a full-screen graphic or card window.
- **Spelling:** caption words are the script words, spelled exactly as in the script. If a voiceover tool mishears a word, the caption keeps the script spelling.
- **Separate file:** for long-form, also export a separate SRT caption file (one caption per phrase, same 2–4 word rule where practical).

## Long-form structure (8–15 minutes)

- **Title and thumbnail promise must match the video's actual payoff.** A mismatch is a retention and trust failure, not a clever hook.
- **Chapters** every 2–3 minutes, with spoken keywords and on-screen chapter titles.
- **Retention checkpoints every 60–90 seconds:** a re-hook, a result, a question, or a new visual — something that earns the next minute. Return to the original premise every 2–3 minutes, visually (a title card) or verbally ("remember, we started with a bitter cup") — the throughline is what holds viewers past minute 8 (REPORTED, AIR study of 100 long-form channels).
- **Captions** throughout (see the spec above).
- **Analytics to review after publishing:** impressions, click-through rate, chose-to-view, average view duration, percentage viewed, retention curve, returning viewers. See the bands below.

### Long-form script template (full worked example)

Topic: "How to brew pour-over coffee at home" — 10-minute tutorial. Full voiceover below: 1,392 words, about 9.3 minutes at 150 wpm (range 8.2–10.7 minutes at 130–170 wpm). Timestamps are targets; confirm with the read-aloud test. Facts used: brew water 92–96°C and ratio 55 g per litre (Specialty Coffee Association, Golden Cup / Standard 310-2021). Everything else is procedure and advice, not factual claims.

**0:00–0:30 — Hook.** On screen: "Bitter coffee? It is not the beans." Spoken: "Your pour-over tastes bitter no matter which beans you buy. The problem is not the bag. It is your grind. In the next ten minutes you will brew a cup that tastes sweet, using three numbers you already own. Stay for the third number, because it is the one most people get wrong."

**0:30–2:00 — Chapter 1: Why coffee tastes bitter.** Spoken: "Bitterness in a pour-over comes from extraction, not from the beans themselves. Extraction is the word for everything the water pulls out of the ground coffee. Pull out too little and the cup tastes sour and thin. Pull out too much and it tastes bitter and dry. Your job is to land in the middle, and there are three levers that move you there: grind size, water temperature, and how you pour. Most bitter cups are over-extracted on one lever and under-extracted on another at the same time, which is why changing the beans never fixes it. The mistake is blaming the beans for a grind problem. Here is how to tell which side you are on. Sour and bitter are not the same fault. Sour means under-extracted: the cup tastes sharp, thin, almost lemony, and the sweetness never arrives. Bitter means over-extracted: the cup tastes dry, harsh, and astringent, and it lingers at the back of your tongue. A cup can be both at once when the grind is uneven, with fine dust over-extracting while coarse chunks under-extract. That is a grinder problem, not a bean problem, and the fix is a more even grind, not a new bag. Before we touch any lever, here is the ratio card you will use for every brew in this video: fifteen grams of coffee to two hundred and fifty grams of water. That is one heaped tablespoon per cup, roughly, and it matches the standard brewing ratio of fifty-five grams per litre. Write it down. Everything after this assumes that ratio." On screen: the three levers as a simple list. Re-hook at 1:30: "The mistake is blaming the beans for a grind problem."

**2:00–4:30 — Chapter 2: Grind size.** Spoken: "Grind size is the first number, and it is the one that matters most. Think of it this way: fine grounds have more surface area, so water extracts from them faster. Coarse grounds have less surface area, so water extracts slower. If your cup is bitter, your grind is probably too fine, and the water is pulling out the bitter compounds along with the sweet ones. Here is the scale to memorise. Coarse, like sea salt, for a slow drawdown — the water takes its time, and the cup stays sweet. Medium, like sand, for a balanced cup — this is where most pour-overs should live. Fine, like table salt, only for a fast brewer, or for a very short contact time. Now test yours with one brew. Grind a batch, rub a pinch between your fingers, and compare it to the scale. If it feels like flour, it is too fine, and that alone explains the bitterness. If it feels like pebbles, it is too coarse, and your cup will taste sour instead. Change one thing at a time. Move the grind one notch coarser, brew again, and taste. Do not change the grind and the temperature on the same day, or you will never know which one fixed it. One notch, one brew, one note in your phone. That is how you learn your grinder instead of guessing at it. Now the full pour, read aloud as you would do it. Start the timer. Pour fifty grams of water, just enough to wet all the grounds, and wait forty-five seconds. This is the bloom. The coffee swells and releases gas, and the bed settles. Then pour to one hundred and fifty grams in slow circles, and wait until the water drops halfway. Pour to two hundred and fifty grams, again in slow circles, keeping the stream on the coffee and off the filter wall. The whole drawdown should finish between two and a half and three minutes. Faster than that, and your grind is too coarse or your pour too aggressive. Slower, and the grind is too fine or the bed is clogged with dust. Time it once, write the time down, and you have a baseline every future brew can be judged against." On screen: each grind size shown as it is explained, then the pour amounts as numbers.

**4:30–6:30 — Chapter 3: Water temperature.** Spoken: "The second number is water temperature, and the range is narrower than most people think. The Specialty Coffee Association puts the brewing range at ninety-two to ninety-six degrees Celsius. In practice, start at ninety-three. That is water just off the boil: boil the kettle, take it off the heat, wait thirty seconds, and pour. Cooler water under-extracts, which is why a cup brewed with lukewarm water tastes sour even when the grind is right. Hotter water extracts faster, which is why boiling water straight onto the grounds can push a cup toward bitter. The number matters because guessing does not work. Your kettle does not know what ninety-three degrees feels like, and neither do you. So use the thirty-second rule, and use it every time. If you own a thermometer, use it for one week. Measure the water at the pour, not in the kettle, because the temperature drops between the kettle and the dripper. You will find your kettle loses two or three degrees in the pour, which is why starting just off the boil lands you in the range instead of below it. After a week you will know your kettle by feel, and the thermometer can go back in the drawer. One more thing about temperature: preheat your dripper and your cup with hot water before you brew. A cold ceramic dripper steals heat from the slurry, and a cup that starts cold drops the temperature of the finished coffee before you taste it. Rinse the paper filter with hot water while you are at it. It warms the dripper and washes out the paper taste in one step. And if your kitchen is cold in the morning, preheat twice. It costs ten seconds and it is the cheapest improvement in this whole video." On screen: the temperature, word for word.

**6:30–8:30 — Chapter 4: What not to do.** Spoken: "Three mistakes ruin more cups than any wrong setting. The first is the stir that disturbs the bed. Once the water is in, leave the grounds alone. Stirring knocks fine particles to the bottom, where they clog the filter, and the brew stalls. A stalled brew over-extracts, and the cup turns bitter. The second is the pour that floods the sides. Water running down the filter wall bypasses the coffee bed entirely. It reaches your cup without extracting anything, so the result is thin and uneven. Pour in slow circles, keep the stream on the coffee, and keep the water level below the rim of the grounds. The third is the extra scoop added out of panic. A weak cup does not need more coffee. It needs a finer grind or hotter water. Adding coffee on top of a bad grind just gives you more of the same bitter cup, stronger. The worst pour-over is the one that adds more coffee before fixing the grind. If a cup disappoints you, change the grind first, then the temperature, then the pour. Never all three at once. And keep the failed cups. Taste them side by side with the next brew and you will learn faster from the comparison than from any single good cup. A notebook with three columns, grind, temperature, and taste, beats a shelf of new beans." Re-hook at 7:30: "The worst pour-over is the one that adds more coffee before fixing the grind."

**8:30–9:30 — Chapter 5: The honest limit.** Spoken: "Here is the limit no technique can cross. Technique cannot save beans that were never fresh. Stale beans, the wrong roast for the method, or beans stored open on a shelf will taste flat no matter how perfect your grind and temperature are. Coffee goes stale because the oils oxidise once the bag is open. You can tell the difference with one test: bloom the grounds with a little hot water and watch. Fresh coffee blooms, it swells and bubbles as the gas escapes. Stale coffee sits flat and still. If your coffee does not bloom, buy a new bag, store it sealed and away from light, and use it within a few weeks of roasting. Fresh beans will not fix a bad grind, but a good grind cannot fix dead beans either."

**9:30–10:00 — Close and call to action.** Spoken: "Three numbers: grind like sand, water at ninety-three degrees, and fifteen grams to two hundred and fifty of water. The brew ratio card is in the description. Copy it, and use it for your next morning cup. That is the moment you will need it."

**Check against the checklist:** hook names the problem in 30 seconds; proof (the three numbers, then the full timed pour) arrives by 2:00; re-hooks at 1:30 and 7:30; one idea per chapter; caveat at 8:30; one call to action; save-worthy asset (the ratio card) with a future-moment anchor ("your next morning cup").

## Discovery and disclosure rules (verified 16 November 2026, re-checked 23 November 2026)

Check YouTube's current help pages before every upload — these rules change. What follows was verified on 16 November 2026 against YouTube's own pages and announcements as reported in November 2026 guides, and re-checked on 23 November 2026 for the Shorts length, view-counting, disclosure, and Content ID items below.

**How Shorts are discovered.** YouTube says Shorts are ranked on performance and viewer personalisation. The signals it names, in order: (1) whether viewers choose to watch or swipe away — Studio shows this as "Viewed vs. swiped away"; (2) whether they stay — average view duration and average percentage viewed; (3) whether they enjoyed it — likes and post-watch survey answers. Personalisation uses each viewer's history: topics watched, channels, and sounds. Topic interest, competition, and seasonality also move a Short's numbers. YouTube says its system has no opinion about what type of Short you make, and there is no minimum posting cadence required to perform well.

**How views are counted.** Since 24 August 2026, a view counts from the first frame of playback across formats. For Shorts analysis, use engaged views (views where the viewer stayed past the first seconds, not counting loops), average view duration, and average percentage viewed — raw views are now an exposure count, not an attention metric.

**AI disclosure.** YouTube requires disclosure when a video contains realistic content that was generated or significantly altered with AI and a viewer could mistake it for real: a real person shown saying or doing something they did not, footage of a real event or place altered, a realistic scene that never happened, or AI-generated music carrying the video. You do **not** need to disclose for: an AI-written script, AI help with a title or thumbnail, filters, colour grading, captions, upscaling, or your own cloned voice used for voiceover. Set it in YouTube Studio at upload, under the "altered or synthetic content" (now labelled "AI use") question. Since 27 May 2026, YouTube may also add the label itself — for content made with YouTube's own AI tools, content carrying C2PA metadata, or content its systems detect as photorealistic AI. The label appears as an overlay on Shorts and below the player on long-form. YouTube says disclosing does not change recommendations or monetisation; failing to disclose realistic content can lead to removal or suspension from the Partner Program.

## Analytics bands (guidance, not platform fact)

YouTube publishes no "good" thresholds. The bands below are practitioner guidance for diagnosing a video — use them to decide what to fix, not as promises. Shorts and long-form are never compared against each other; compare a video against your own channel's median for the same format.

| Metric (Shorts) | Strong | Average | Weak — fix this |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average percentage viewed | Over 80% | 50–80% | Under 50% — tighten the hook and cut dead beats |
| Chose-to-view ("Viewed vs. swiped away") | Over 70% | 50–70% | Under 50% — the first 2 seconds fail |
| Average view duration (60s Short) | Over 40s | 25–40s | Under 25s — viewers leave before the steps |
| Likes per 100 views | Over 4 | 1–4 | Under 1 — the payoff or the ask is weak |

| Metric (long-form) | Strong | Average | Weak — fix this |
|---|---|---|---|
| Click-through rate | Over 8% | 3–8% | Under 3% — rework title and thumbnail |
| Average percentage viewed | Over 50% | 30–50% | Under 30% — restructure the opening |
| Audience retention at 30s | Over 70% | 50–70% | Under 50% — the hook does not pay off |

Judge by rates, not raw counts. A video with 200 views and 80% average viewed teaches you more than a video with 20,000 views and 25% viewed. Wait for at least 24 hours after publishing before reading retention — YouTube Analytics lags, and early numbers are noisy. Do not explain weak numbers with "shadowban", "probation", or "test pool" theories; the data is the data. One more diagnostic: when the retention curve drops at the same second across three videos, fix that second in the next video — the drop is a script defect at that timestamp, not an audience mood.

## Failure modes

- **Hook is a greeting.** Fix: cut everything before the problem sentence.
- **Proof arrives after 15 seconds.** Fix: move the demonstration up; cut the setup.
- **Two calls to action.** Fix: keep the one that matches the video's promise; cut the other.
- **Script reads fine but sounds written.** Fix: run the read-aloud test; rewrite any sentence you stumble on.
- **Captions transcribe everything.** Fix: shorten each caption to the keyword; delete captions that duplicate the voiceover word for word.
- **A claim with no source.** Fix: find the source or cut the claim. Never ship it on trust.
- **Script runs long.** Fix: check whether you counted the spoken headline in the budget — the budget covers all spoken words. Cut from the middle steps first, never from the caveat.

## Known limits
